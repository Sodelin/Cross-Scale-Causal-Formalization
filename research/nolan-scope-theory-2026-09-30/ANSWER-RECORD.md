## 3. Conceptual analysis after Nolan's correction

No—I had not fully broken down your theory, and I had not developed or validated an entire theory of my own. **I implemented part of your proposed remedy before adequately explaining the idea behind it.** Your criticism is accurate. The earlier work was useful, but it did not answer the conceptual question you were asking.

I’ve now written and published the [analysis of your theory](https://github.com/Sodelin/Formalizing-Soft-Sciences/blob/main/research/new-wave-2026-09-30/NOLAN-THEORY-ANALYSIS.md). It distinguishes your original hypothesis from my proposed mathematical interpretation. Here is the substantive explanation.

**I understand your central claim as a failure in constructing the search space.** An assistant can solve a problem correctly, improve the answer, and verify every step while never representing the more consequential question. When you name the missing dimension—“Why not all levels?”—you change what the search considers. The issue therefore begins before correctness checking.

Your two scope failures are different:

- **Within the task:** the assistant represents selected parts of the system while omitting something necessary for the intended outcome.
- **Beyond the task:** it misses the larger structure, neighboring applications, or surrounding dependencies that make a result more general or useful.

Checking the existing answer can inherit either omission. Asking several agents to review the same narrow formulation does not automatically fix it.

A simple formal distinction captures this. Let \(T\) be a specified space of possible targets, and let \(C\subseteq T\) be the candidates the current representation makes available. Finding the best answer within \(C\) does not establish that it is best within \(T\). If an important target never enters \(C\), more comparisons inside \(C\) cannot select it.

That is my proposed mathematical interpretation of your argument—not proof that we can directly observe a language model’s internal candidate set.

This also clarifies what an intervention should do. **It must generate a changed statement or representation, rather than certify that “generalization was considered.”** My proposed mechanism is:

1. Recover the properties that the decisive argument actually uses.
2. Remove restrictions it does not use.
3. Where it uses a restriction, investigate whether a weaker structural property would suffice.
4. Look for another class possessing those recovered properties.
5. Test the resulting alternative with a derivation, counterexample, source investigation, or dependency trace.

This addresses your hardest question: how could I discover a dimension without you knowing its name first? The procedure starts from dependencies in the argument, rather than requiring a predetermined list of words such as *level*, *size*, or *noise*.

For NANUQ, that could produce: “What controls the size of a local obstruction, and does that size depend on the global network parameter?” The six-label dependence then makes the all-level target visible. The restriction and representation arguments still need proving; absence of “level” from a formula alone is insufficient.

Your **“shadow” idea** gives a second formal direction. A shadow can be an observation of an underlying system, or the structure preserved when mapping one task into another. We can ask precisely which properties survive that mapping.

The important limitation is that different hidden systems can produce the same shadow. Matching observations can support some conclusions without identifying the underlying mechanism. For psychology, this turns your idea into a productive question: **which properties are recoverable from behavior, and which additional measurements or interventions would distinguish otherwise indistinguishable mechanisms?**

Could this be formalized in Lean? Yes, in meaningful parts: the distinction between local success and outcome coverage, limitations of a specified candidate generator, preservation under transfer, and reductions from unbounded problems to bounded obstructions. **Lean could not make an instruction control my behavior or prove that I found every worthwhile target.** Encoding “perfect search” as an assumption would conceal the very problem we want to solve.

My assessment is that your theory contains a useful, formalizable account of **missed scope and target generation**. Its reward-based causal explanation remains a hypothesis. The proposed generative intervention is also unvalidated; we need matched trials to establish whether it improves consequential results per resource used.

The report provides the analysis I previously skipped. It is a developed proposal, not a completed empirical theory or an exhaustive research corpus.
